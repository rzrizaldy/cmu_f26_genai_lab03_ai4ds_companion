Claim 1 · statistical testSquad A · born Jan–Apr

Did the Mens email work?

Answer · holds
Yes: +7.7 points in visits, about +$0.77 per customer.
The split is fine (each group got about a third, SRM p = 0.90). The 95% range for spend is $0.49 to $1.06. With 21,000 people per group almost anything is "significant", so the size and the range are the numbers to report. Spend is $0 for 99% of people, which is why the t-test, Mann-Whitney and a bootstrap answer slightly different questions.
Claim 2 · econometricsSquad B · born May–Aug

Which controls belong in the model?

Emails were assigned at random, so comparing the groups is already causal. The analyst added controls. Turning on visit means: compare only people who all visited, or all didn't.

other controls:
Answer · wrong
Visit is the road the email travels on. Blocking it hides the effect.
With visit in the model the Mens effect is $0.22; without it, $0.77. Visiting happens after the email and partly because of it, so it is a bad control. Every control has to be measured before the treatment. The other three controls barely move anything, which is what randomization buys you.
Claim 3 · multiple testingSquad C · born Sep–Dec

A winning subgroup, or a winning lottery ticket?

The analyst ran the same test (Womens email vs no email, on buying) in 18 subgroups and reported the best one. Each ticket is one test.

chance that at least one of 18 tests still looks "significant" at p < 0.05. That's 1 − 0.9518.

Answer · a hypothesis for now
The ticket might be real, but the memo hid how many were bought.
Urban / Web / new customers still passes the Bonferroni cutoff (0.05 / 18 = 0.0028), so it may be a real effect. The memo never said 18 groups were compared. A target found by searching should be tested again in the next campaign before budget moves to it.

The task said each email costs $0.10, but the objective only adds up uplift. With the cost included, a segment whose uplift is below $0.10 loses money. Our solver includes it.

Answer · the promise is too big
The plan delivers about half of what it promises, in 97% of birthdays.
Planning "carefully" on the low end makes the promise smaller, but it still delivers about half of it. The optimizer picks segments whose estimates were lucky, so only fresh data tells you what a plan is worth. The plan still beats emailing 20,000 random customers. Next time, keep a random holdout group in the send to measure it.

Five real tasks. Pick a winner on each card, then it flips.

Could a person answer from one row?Then the knowledge is in language and common sense, and JEV already has it. Use it with no labels, calibrate it with a few, or let it label data for a small free model.
Does it need patterns from many rows?Then the signal lives in your data, and only training finds it. Use classic ML (XGBoost, CatBoost).
Answer · wrong for this task
On these customers JEV scores like a coin flip (AUC 0.52).
XGBoost trained on 42,194 customers reaches 0.61. Keep our own model for customers, and use JEV for text such as reviews and complaints, where it beat a model trained on 22,134 labels.

The traffic alert pages you for the campaign and sleeps through the outage.
